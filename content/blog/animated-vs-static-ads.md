Animated vs static ads has no universal performance winner: Animoto's Facebook tests found that an image beat one video, while another video performed better.[^1] My recommendation is to test animation when movement explains a benefit, and static when a still image communicates the offer clearly. Choose using qualified outcomes and total production-plus-media cost.

*By Nolan Grout, founder of Komendly.*

For a small business, the practical question is what to make next. This guide separates the published evidence from my recommendations, then gives you a checklist and experiment brief for making that decision.

## What is the difference between animated and static ads?

A static ad presents a still visual. An animated ad introduces designed movement: a headline appearing, an illustration changing, or a product moving through a sequence.

For this guide, **motion ads** means creative built around animation. **Video ads** is the broader category, including filmed demonstrations, interviews, and other moving-image executions.

Keep those definitions in mind when reading the research below. Evidence about a filmed demonstration does not establish what will happen with a gently animated product image. Ask whether the execution being studied resembles the creative you intend to make.

Use this table to choose a starting hypothesis. The strengths, limitations, and verdicts are editorial judgments, not performance benchmarks.

| Format | Suggested strength | Limitation to consider | My best-for verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Static image | Keeps a single proposition visible in one composition | Cannot demonstrate movement within the still image | Start here for an offer that makes sense at a glance |
| Simple animation | Can show a change or sequence | Requires decisions about timing and what appears when | Test it when movement explains a specific benefit |
| Filmed video | Lets you show a real demonstration or explanation | Requires suitable footage and production planning | Consider it when seeing the actual process matters |

Give each format a communication job before giving it a budget.

## Do video ads beat image ads?

Sometimes. In Animoto's experiments published by [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/to-video-or-not-to-video), an optimized image generated more clicks at a lower cost than a listicle video. A narrative video performed better than that image.[^1]

My interpretation is that this supports testing strong executions on both sides. Comparing a carefully written animation with an unrelated photo would leave you asking whether the difference came from movement, relevance, or the underlying message.

### Where did static images win?

Northcott and colleagues compared Facebook image and video advertising for a physical-activity smartphone app.[^2] Their [accepted manuscript](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/941d9402-29d8-4ea3-aafc-7c816572bb41/content) reports click-through rates of 2.7% for images and 1.9% for videos.[^2] App-download rates were 0.6% for images and 0.5% for videos.[^2]

Treat those findings as results from that campaign. Do not use them as a forecast for your shop, service, or software business.

### Where did video win?

Ben Raue's account of a GetUp experiment, published by the [Commons Social Change Library](https://commonslibrary.org/experiment-facebook-videos-vs-graphics-for-conversions/), reports 300 conversions for a Facebook video and 148 for a still graphic.[^3] The author explicitly describes the result as not definitive.[^3]

Before borrowing its conclusion, ask what counted as a conversion and whether that action resembles your own goal. A reported conversion count alone is not enough for your budgeting decision; establish what a qualified outcome means for your business.

**My reading of these examples:** they justify a format test, not a blanket promise that adding movement will improve sales.

## When should you test animation instead of another static image?

Start with an unfinished explanation. What does the customer need to understand that your current image does not show?

For a hypothetical furniture business, that might be how a folding desk opens. For a software business, it might be the transition from an input to an output. For a local service, it might be the steps from requesting a quote to arranging an appointment.

Write the proposed contribution of motion in a sentence:

- Show the desk changing from its stored position to its usable position.
- Demonstrate the action that produces the advertised result.
- Reveal the steps involved in getting started.

If your explanation is simply “make it more eye-catching,” refine the hypothesis. Specify what someone should notice and why it matters to their decision.

Include the audience's awareness stage in the brief. Ask whether you are introducing a problem, explaining a solution, or presenting an offer to someone who already knows the product. For messaging examples, see [ads for the stages of awareness](/blog/awareness-stages-ad-examples).

My starting recommendation: choose static when your strongest selling point fits clearly in a still composition. Test motion when you can name the information that movement adds.

## How can the MOVE checklist help you choose?

Use the **MOVE checklist** before committing to animation. This is an editorial planning tool, not a validated predictor of ad performance.

### M: Does movement communicate the message?

Complete this sentence: “The movement helps explain…”

Look for a concrete answer, such as how the product works or what changes during use. If you cannot finish the sentence, draft a clearer static concept first.

Review the proposed opening composition as well. Decide what someone should understand before the sequence develops.

### O: Is the outcome defined?

Choose the business outcome before approving the creative. For a lead campaign, write down your definition of a qualified lead. For a sales campaign, decide how you will evaluate purchases and acquisition cost.

Also define disqualifying outcomes. If you only serve a particular area, decide how you will classify inquiries from elsewhere. Apply the same rule to both versions.

Avoid choosing a winner using whichever metric looks strongest afterward.

### V: Are the versions comparable?

Give the static and animated versions the same proposition, evidence, destination, and call to action. Use the same product and a comparable visual treatment.

If motion introduces a different offer or story, call the experiment a comparison of concepts. That can still be useful; write the question accurately.

Make each execution persuasive on its own terms. Do not use an awkward animation frame as the static version merely to make the assets look similar.

### E: Can the economics support the experiment?

Estimate production effort and reserve a separate testing budget. Include revisions, review time, and preparation of the required layouts.

Proceed when you can explain the movement, define the outcome, prepare comparable versions, and afford the test you designed. Resolve a missing condition before expanding production.

## Are animated ads worth it for small budgets?

My recommendation is to test a focused animation when movement has a clear explanatory job and production leaves enough budget to evaluate results.

Avoid commissioning a large batch before deciding what the first experiment should teach you. Prepare a production estimate using your own time and expenses:

| Cost category | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Concept and copy | Work spent choosing the angle, offer, and wording |
| Initial production | Design or editing time and direct expenses |
| Revisions | Corrections, approvals, and replacement assets |
| Layout preparation | Work needed for each intended composition |
| Media | Advertising spend allocated to the experiment |

Assign a value to your own time, even when you do the work yourself. For broader budgeting context, see [Facebook advertising costs for small businesses](/blog/facebook-ads-cost-small-business).

### How should you calculate the trade-off?

Use this management calculation alongside your campaign reporting:

**Total cost per qualified outcome = total media and allocated production cost divided by qualified outcomes.**

Calculate it separately for the static and animated versions. Use the same qualification rules and cost-allocation method.

Then ask what would justify the animation's production commitment. Would it need to generate more qualified outcomes, more valuable orders, or sufficient reuse to meet your cost target? Write that requirement before reviewing results.

If a version produces no qualified outcomes, report that result directly. Do not calculate a finite cost per qualified outcome by substituting clicks or unqualified inquiries.

Keep projected reuse separate from costs already incurred. Decide whether you are evaluating the initial experiment or a defined period of reuse, and use that scope consistently.

## How should you test animated vs static ads?

Use a written experiment brief. The following is my recommended process for comparing a specific static execution with a specific animated execution.

### What is your hypothesis?

For example: “Showing the desk opening will reduce cost per qualified purchase compared with showing the same desk already open.”

That statement identifies the movement, comparison, and decision metric. Keep the eventual conclusion equally specific.

### Which details should stay consistent?

Keep the offer, product, supporting evidence, destination, and call to action consistent. Let the animated version demonstrate the proposed movement without adding a separate selling argument.

Prepare the required layouts before launch. Use the [Facebook and Instagram ad sizes guide](/blog/facebook-instagram-ad-sizes) as a planning reference.

Review both assets for legibility, accuracy, and whether the intended proposition is clear. Ask a colleague to describe the offer without explaining it to them first.

### What should you document before launch?

Record the audience, delivery choices, measurement method, budget, and schedule you intend to use. If you run the experiment through Meta Ads Manager, save a record of the settings applied to each version.

Write down any differences you intentionally allow. If you change both format and placement strategy, describe the experiment as a comparison of those combined choices.

For the narrowest question about movement, seek an experiment design with randomized, non-overlapping groups. If you cannot adequately control exposure, keep that limitation in your interpretation.

### When should you stop the test?

Set the spending limit, planned duration, acceptable acquisition cost, and evidence requirements before launch. Allow for your purchase cycle and the time needed to qualify inquiries.

Avoid stopping merely because one version takes an early lead. Equally, do not continue spending beyond a limit you established to protect the business.

A small budget may leave the question unresolved. Accept that possibility in the brief rather than promising a winner.

### Which results should determine the next step?

For a sales or lead-generation campaign, my recommendation is to prioritize qualified outcomes and their total cost. Use clicks and other intermediate measures to investigate what needs attention.

If an ad attracts clicks without qualified outcomes, review the promise, destination, and qualification process together. Resist assigning the entire result to format.

Choose the next action explicitly: expand the promising execution, revise a specific weakness, or collect more evidence.

## What should you do when results are inconclusive?

First, check the experiment's integrity. Were the intended versions delivered? Did measurement work? Were leads classified consistently?

Then distinguish limited evidence from an unacceptable business result. If the evidence requirements in your brief were not met, record the comparison as inconclusive. If they were met but neither execution achieved your business target, reconsider the offer or concept before producing more variants.

For suspected creative fatigue, write a fresh hypothesis. Decide whether the next test concerns a new message, offer, or execution, and preserve a recognizable comparison.

If your budget cannot support further testing, choose the option with the more manageable production commitment. Document the uncertainty so a temporary budgeting choice does not become an unsupported rule about all animated ads.
