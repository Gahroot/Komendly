Video testimonials deserve a collection process that avoids six mistakes: complicated requests, arbitrary timing, vague questions, edits that change meaning, publishing without a reuse plan, and choosing stories unrelated to the buyer’s question. Start with a specific experience, let the customer describe it honestly, document permission, and give each finished video a clear purpose.

I recommend treating testimonial collection as an editorial process. Decide what you want to learn, ask useful questions, check the resulting story, and choose where it belongs. A camera and a satisfied customer are the starting point; neither replaces that work.

The workflow below is my practical recommendation, not a promise of higher conversion rates. Use it to build a collection system you can evaluate against your own business goals.

## Which six mistakes weaken your video testimonials?

### 1. Making the request too complicated

Review your invitation from the customer’s side. Does it explain what you are asking for, what participation involves, and where you would like to use the finished video? If those details are missing, rewrite the request before sending it.

I would keep the initial invitation focused on a single experience. Ask whether the customer would be comfortable answering a few questions about a completed project, a purchase, or a particular use of your product.

Then provide a short recording brief:

- Explain the topic you would like to discuss.
- Share the questions in advance.
- Offer simple recording instructions or an interview option.
- Describe the proposed publication channels.
- Make declining an acceptable response.

For recording, suggest a quiet room, a stable phone, and a quick sound check. Treat those as practical instructions, not evidence that smartphone footage performs better than professional production.

**What to do instead:** Give customers a clear next step and enough information to make an informed choice. Provide prompts rather than a script praising your business.

### 2. Choosing an arbitrary time to ask

Replace a universal waiting period with a question: has this customer had enough experience to describe something meaningful?

For a service business, you might choose project completion. For a product business, you might choose a follow-up after the customer has used the purchase. For software, you might choose the completion of a workflow the customer originally wanted to improve.

These are suggested collection milestones, not proven timing rules. Choose one that fits your business and review whether customers can answer your questions at that point.

Assign responsibility for the invitation. Record whether it was sent, whether the customer replied, and whether they want further contact. Set a respectful follow-up policy rather than repeatedly chasing a recording.

Keep private feedback and public participation as separate requests. Let customers explain what needs improvement without requiring them to agree to appear in marketing.

**What to do instead:** Write down the milestone, the person responsible, and the follow-up process. Review the process when customers repeatedly say they cannot yet evaluate the experience.

### 3. Asking for praise instead of a specific experience

Start with questions about events and decisions. Avoid asking customers to repeat your positioning or confirm a result you have already written for them.

Try these prompts:

- What were you trying to accomplish?
- What did you use or do before this?
- Which part of the experience would you describe to someone considering it?
- What changed, if anything?
- What should a prospective customer know before deciding?

If someone mentions a measurable result, ask what they measured, over which period, and what else changed. Keep those details with the recording. Do not turn a customer’s estimate into a verified business result.

FTC guidance says endorsements must reflect honest opinions and cannot make claims the advertiser could not legally make.[^1] Treat the testimonial review as a claim review, not just a grammar check.

For exceptional outcomes, the FTC says advertisers without evidence that the result is representative must clearly communicate generally expected results.[^1] Do not automatically turn one customer’s experience into a headline promising everyone the same outcome.

**What to do instead:** Ask for context and concrete details. Publish only the claims you can support, with the qualifications needed to explain them accurately.

### 4. Editing away the customer’s meaning

Set an editing brief before opening the footage. Aim to remove distractions, make the story understandable, and preserve what the customer actually meant.

Keep the source recording alongside the edited version. Check whether a cut removes a qualification, whether a headline adds certainty, or whether rearranging sentences changes the sequence of events.

FTC endorsement guidance says rewording or presenting an endorsement out of context must not distort the endorser’s opinion or experience.[^4]

For example, check whether you have removed a mention of extra support, a limited use case, or a condition attached to a result. Ask the customer to review the proposed edit, including the caption and headline that will appear with it.

Also review accessibility. W3C explains that captions make audio information accessible to people who are deaf or hard of hearing.[^3] Its guidance explains that captions include dialogue, speaker identification, and meaningful non-speech sounds.[^3]

**What to do instead:** Keep an original, an edited version, and a record of approval. Check captions against the audio rather than treating an automatic transcript as finished work.

### 5. Publishing without a reuse plan

Choose an initial placement before making additional versions. Write down the question that the testimonial should help a visitor evaluate.

A product page might need an account of using a particular feature. A service page might need a description of the working relationship. A sales presentation might need an example relevant to the prospect’s situation. These are editorial choices to test, not guaranteed conversion tactics.

For each approved testimonial, record:

- The original recording and transcript.
- The customer’s approved attribution.
- The agreed publication channels.
- The specific claims and supporting context.
- The approved edits and accompanying disclosures.
- The locations where each version is published.

FTC guidance says unexpected connections that could affect the weight given to an endorsement should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously.[^1] Include that check when preparing each placement.

If you are starting from a written review, assess the proposed marketing use separately. FTC guidance says consumer reviews become testimonials when a business features them in advertising or marketing materials.[^2]

**What to do instead:** Build a small, organized library. Make each new placement a deliberate editorial decision, with a check of permission, wording, context, and presentation.

### 6. Choosing stories without considering the buyer’s question

Before selecting a testimonial, finish this sentence: this story belongs here because the visitor is trying to understand a particular issue.

That issue might be the work involved in getting started, the experience of receiving support, or suitability for a particular task. Choose a story that actually addresses it. Do not add a claim merely because the placement needs one.

Organize your collection by useful questions rather than by the strength of the praise. Suggested categories include implementation, day-to-day use, service experience, and limitations the customer wants others to understand.

For awareness stages, use the buyer’s immediate question as your editorial guide. A problem-aware visitor might need context about the original difficulty. A product-aware visitor might want an account of using the product. Treat those choices as hypotheses to evaluate.

**What to do instead:** Match each placement to a relevant, approved story. If the library does not contain one, leave the gap visible in your content plan rather than stretching an unrelated testimonial.

## How should you execute a repeatable collection process?

Use my **CLEAR checklist** before publishing. It is an editorial review tool, not a validated predictor of advertising performance.

| Check | Question to answer | Suggested action if incomplete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consent | Have we documented the customer’s agreement to this use? | Confirm the intended channels and attribution. |
| Literal meaning | Does the edit preserve the original message? | Compare it with the source recording. |
| Evidence | Can we support the claims we plan to publish? | Verify, qualify, or remove unsupported statements. |
| Accessibility | Have we checked the captions and presentation? | Review the final version before release. |
| Relevance | Does this story address the intended buyer’s question? | Choose another placement or another story. |

Assign an owner to each unfinished item. Keep a testimonial in review until those items are resolved. This gives your team an explicit publication decision rather than a folder of vaguely approved footage.

Start with a manageable pilot. Choose a customer milestone, prepare the questions, send the invitations, and review the resulting material using CLEAR. Record where the process stalls. Revise the invitation if expectations are unclear, or revise the questions if the answers lack context.

## What does a weak collection process cost your business?

Evaluate your own process instead of assigning it an unsupported revenue penalty. Track invitations, replies, approved stories, revision work, and published placements. Use those records to decide what needs attention.

Keep collection outcomes separate from business outcomes. An approved testimonial is a completed asset; decide separately whether its placement contributes to the result you care about, such as qualified inquiries or purchases.

When evaluating a placement, document what else changed. Avoid attributing every improvement to a testimonial added during a wider page redesign. Where practical, plan a comparison before changing the page, and be cautious when drawing conclusions from limited data.

My recommended starting point is one approved story answering one specific buyer question. Complete the permission, evidence, editing, and accessibility checks, then evaluate its use before expanding the process.

## What else should you know about collecting video testimonials?

### How often should I collect video testimonials?

Choose a recurring review schedule and a meaningful customer milestone. Ask when customers can describe the experience rather than setting a universal weekly quota.

### Can I use text testimonials instead of video?

Yes. Choose the format for the placement and the customer’s preferences. Evaluate it against your own goals without assuming a fixed advantage for video.

### How should I prepare testimonial interview questions?

Provide questions and recording instructions rather than predetermined praise. Ask for the customer’s own account and approval of the final wording.

### What if a customer does not want to appear on camera?

Offer a written response, discuss another comfortable format, or accept a refusal. Keep the invitation optional.

### Can I turn a written review into an AI-avatar testimonial?

Komendly turns text reviews into AI-avatar videos. Preserve the review’s meaning, obtain permission for the proposed use, and clearly identify the avatar presentation. Remember the FTC’s warning that avatar use can still be deceptive.[^2]

### Can I edit a video testimonial?

Preserve meaning and context. FTC endorsement guidance says edits must not distort the endorser’s opinion or experience.[^4]
