Stages of awareness ads change their opening message according to what the buyer already knows: the problem, possible solutions, or your product.[^1] For someone unfamiliar with the problem, begin with a relevant situation; for someone considering your product, answer their doubts; for someone ready to act, explain the offer.[^3] Below, you’ll find an example angle, headline, visual, and next step for each stage.

*By Nolan Grout, founder of Komendly.*

## What are the 5 stages of awareness?

Eugene Schwartz introduced the awareness framework in *Breakthrough Advertising*.[^1] Use the Eugene Schwartz awareness levels to decide how much explanation to put before your offer.

| Awareness stage | What the buyer understands | Suggested starting point for your ad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Has not recognized the problem.[^1] | A familiar moment or observation |
| Problem aware | Recognizes the problem, but not a solution.[^1] | Name the difficulty clearly |
| Solution aware | Knows the desired result, but may not know your product provides it.[^3] | Explain your approach |
| Product aware | Knows what you sell, but is unsure it fits.[^3] | Answer a specific buying question |
| Most aware | Knows your product and needs the deal.[^3] | State the offer and next step |

Use the final column as a writing recommendation, not a rule that guarantees results. Your task is to choose a sensible opening, then find out whether it connects with the people you want to reach.

For the examples, imagine **Example Kitchen**, a fictional local meal-prep service offering ready-to-heat dinners, a weekly menu, and local delivery. All its product details and ad copy below are invented for this demonstration. These are not real campaigns, customer quotations, or performance results.

## What should an unaware ad look like?

For an unaware ad, try beginning with a moment the reader can recognize before introducing a product.

### Example angle: the evening that disappears

- **Headline:** “Home from work. Still deciding what’s for dinner?”
- **Supporting copy:** “First the fridge check. Then the recipe search. Then another trip for ingredients. Take a look at a different way to plan weeknight dinners.”
- **Visual:** A short animated sequence showing a clock, an open fridge, and an unfinished shopping list.
- **Next step:** “Explore a different dinner routine.”

In this example, the opening describes a situation without assuming the reader wants meal delivery. The next page could walk through dinner-planning options and introduce prepared meals as one possibility.

Keep the story tied to the service you eventually intend to sell. A funny kitchen mishap could be an entertaining opening, but ask whether it creates a useful bridge to the offer. Brian Clark similarly recommends that attention-getting content for unaware readers stay relevant to the business’s ultimate goal.[^3]

**Before publishing:** Read the headline without the image. Does it describe a specific moment, or could almost any business use it?

## What should a problem-aware ad look like?

For a problem-aware example, name the difficulty directly and offer a practical way to investigate it.

### Example angle: dinner is another task after work

- **Headline:** “Tired of planning dinner after a full workday?”
- **Supporting copy:** “Compare batch cooking, meal kits, and prepared dinners. Choose the approach you would actually want to use on a busy evening.”
- **Visual:** A kitchen counter with a shopping list beside a simple dinner-planning checklist.
- **Next step:** “Find your dinner-planning approach.”

The opening assumes the reader already recognizes the frustration. It does not assume they have selected prepared meals as the answer.

For this fictional campaign, send the reader to an honest comparison of approaches. Include reasons someone might prefer cooking from scratch. Then explain where Example Kitchen’s ready-to-heat meals fit.

Avoid turning the problem into an accusation. Compare “You’re wasting your evenings” with “Still planning dinner after work?” My preference is the second: it names the situation while leaving the reader room to decide whether it applies.

**Before publishing:** Underline the exact problem in your copy. If you cannot find it, revise the opening before adding more product information.

## What should a solution-aware ad look like?

For a solution-aware example, introduce your approach and make it concrete enough to evaluate.

### Example angle: prepared dinners instead of another recipe

- **Headline:** “Ready-to-heat dinners for your workweek.”
- **Supporting copy:** “Example Kitchen prepares the meals and delivers locally. Browse the weekly menu and decide whether the dinners fit your routine.”
- **Visual:** A meal moving from its container onto a dinner plate, with a menu preview alongside it.
- **Next step:** “See the weekly menu.”

Here, the ad focuses on the proposed solution: prepared food rather than ingredients or recipes. The destination should make that approach tangible through the actual menu and an explanation of how the fictional service works.

For your own business, choose a distinction you can demonstrate. A cleaning service might explain its appointment process. A software business might show the task being completed. Write around what you can substantiate rather than adding an unsupported “best” or “fastest.”

**Before publishing:** Finish this sentence: “Our approach is suitable when you want…” If your answer is only “better results,” make it more specific.

## What should a product-aware ad look like?

For a product-aware example, select one unresolved question about the named product and answer it.

### Example angle: inspect the menu before deciding

- **Headline:** “Wondering what’s on Example Kitchen’s menu?”
- **Supporting copy:** “Take a closer look at this week’s dinners, ingredients, and delivery information before choosing your meals.”
- **Visual:** A readable menu preview paired with photographs of the corresponding dishes.
- **Next step:** “Explore this week’s meals.”

For this fictional example, assume those details are available on the destination page. In a real campaign, confirm the page contains everything the ad promises.

Choose the question from customer research. Joanna Wiebe recommends open-ended conversations with current customers, former customers, and prospects to understand their motivations and language.[^2] Ask what they still needed to know before deciding, then use the answer to draft your ad.

A useful brief might read: “This reader knows our service but wants to inspect the food.” That is more actionable than “Make a convincing brand ad.”

**Before publishing:** Name the doubt your ad addresses. If you have listed several, choose one for the opening and move supporting details onto the destination page.

## What should a most-aware ad look like?

For a most-aware example, present the actual offer plainly and give the reader a direct action.

### Example angle: choose the dinners

- **Headline:** “Choose your Example Kitchen dinners for next week.”
- **Supporting copy:** “Review the current menu, select your meals, and check the delivery details before ordering.”
- **Visual:** The available meals with a clear ordering prompt.
- **Next step:** “Choose your meals.”

This version does not repeat the earlier explanation of why dinner planning can be frustrating. It asks for the decision the fictional reader is ready to make.

You do not need to invent a discount to write this example. Use the real offer. If there is a genuine ordering deadline, state it accurately. If there is no deadline, leave urgency out.

For your own ad, place material conditions close to the offer. Then inspect the destination from the reader’s perspective: can they find the advertised option and understand what happens next?

**Before publishing:** Remove any sentence that repeats an explanation this particular reader is assumed to know. Keep the information they need to act responsibly.

## Which awareness stage should I target first?

My recommendation is to start with the stage you can support with the clearest customer evidence. Do not automatically begin with unaware messaging because it appears first in the framework.

Use this decision tree as a planning aid:

1. **Are people asking how to buy your specific offer?** Draft a most-aware ad that presents the real offer and action.
2. **Are they asking whether your product fits their needs?** Draft a product-aware ad around the unresolved question.
3. **Are they comparing ways to solve the problem?** Start with a solution-aware explanation of your approach.
4. **Are they describing the problem without proposing an approach?** Test a problem-aware opening.
5. **Do you lack evidence that they recognize the problem?** Try an unaware opening built around a relevant situation, and treat the assignment as provisional.

These are hypotheses for choosing copy, not proof of what any individual thinks.

If you have no customer conversations yet, begin by collecting them. Ask what prompted the search, what alternatives the person considered, and what they needed to understand before deciding. Record their actual words separately from your interpretation.

For a small initial campaign, I would choose a primary stage and an adjacent alternative rather than draft every possible angle immediately. Write down why you chose them so you can revisit the assumption after the test.

## How can you turn awareness stages into a usable creative brief?

Use **KNOW**, an original checklist for this guide. It connects your stage hypothesis to an ad you can review.

### K: What does the buyer know?

Write one sentence describing the assumed starting point. For Example Kitchen: “The reader wants prepared dinners but has not considered this service.” Add the customer evidence supporting that assumption.

### N: What is their next question?

Choose the question this ad will answer. Examples include “What approach could help?” or “What would I actually receive?” Keep the question narrow enough to answer with the available space.

### O: What observable evidence can you offer?

Specify what the reader will see: a menu, a demonstration, an explanation, or documented offer terms. Remove claims you cannot support. For fictional drafts, mark invented details clearly before anyone mistakes them for approved copy.

### W: What will you ask them to do?

Choose the action and destination together. If the ad promises a menu, send readers to the menu. If it invites them to inspect an example, make that example easy to find.

Review the headline, visual, supporting copy, and destination against the same brief. Joanna Wiebe also recommends matching landing-page messaging to awareness rather than sending every ad to the same page.[^2]

## How should you test stages of awareness ads?

Treat the first test as a question about your messaging hypothesis.

For example: “For this audience, should our opening name the dinner-planning problem or introduce ready-to-heat meals?” Draft those alternatives before polishing either design.

My suggested testing worksheet has these fields:

- **Hypothesis:** The question you want the test to answer.
- **Versions:** The messages you plan to compare.
- **Conditions:** The audience, offer, destination, and other details you intend to keep consistent.
- **Decision measure:** The business action you will use to assess the result.
- **Limitations:** Any differences that could complicate your interpretation.

If you change the message and destination together, record that you are comparing complete routes. Avoid attributing the outcome solely to the headline.

Keep this worksheet beside your creative brief during campaign planning. For production specifications, consult the [Facebook and Instagram ad sizes guide](/blog/facebook-instagram-ad-sizes).

If results disappoint, inspect the whole route before assigning blame to the awareness stage. Check that the message is clear, the promised information exists, and the requested action is possible. Treat any explanation as something to investigate rather than a proven diagnosis.
