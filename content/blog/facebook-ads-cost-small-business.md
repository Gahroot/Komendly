For Facebook ads, WordStream’s 2026 US benchmarks report **$0.60 per click for traffic campaigns** and **$27.39 per lead for leads campaigns**, as of November 2026.[^1] A small business should treat these as planning references, then set its budget using an affordable acquisition cost, a defined test goal, and separate creative and management expenses.

My recommendation is to work backward from the business result you need. Before choosing a daily allowance, write down what you can afford to lose, what would count as a useful outcome, and what decision the test should help you make.

## What costs should a small business include?

Build a worksheet with separate lines for media, production and management. This is the accounting structure I recommend for planning an experiment:

| Budget category | What to enter |
| --- | --- |
| Media | Your planned spending on Facebook ad distribution |
| Creative production | Your actual design, copy, animation and production expenses |
| Software | Subscriptions or purchases you allocate to the test |
| Management | Paid setup, monitoring and reporting work |
| Your time | Hours you want to account for separately |

Use actual quotes, invoices and spending records wherever possible. Label estimates clearly so you can replace them after the test.

Keep the media result and the business result in separate columns. For example, record qualified inquiries and paying customers alongside your advertising measurements. Define what makes an inquiry qualified before spending begins.

## What do the Facebook advertising benchmarks actually measure?

WordStream’s benchmark campaigns ran from April 2025 through June 2026. Its reported averages are medians, and its currency values are USD.[^1]

For measurement terminology, Hootsuite’s Facebook and Instagram documentation defines **CPM** as average cost per 1,000 impressions, **CPC** as average cost per click, and **CPL** as average cost per lead.[^3]

Keep those units visible in your worksheet. When reviewing a click benchmark, ask a click question. When reviewing customer acquisition, use your customer records and the expenses you have chosen to include.

Also check the exact click metric. Hootsuite documents that **Clicks (all)** can include link clicks, reactions, comments and shares.[^3] For a website-focused test, I recommend recording website activity separately rather than treating every interaction as a visit.

## How can you calculate a budget using the Margin-First Test?

My **Margin-First Test** is a planning checklist: establish an affordable acquisition target, choose the evidence you want to collect, and set a firm spending ceiling. The equations below are planning tools, not market benchmarks or predictions.

### What can you afford to acquire a customer?

Start with your sales and fulfillment records. Deduct the variable costs associated with delivering a sale, then decide how much room you need for overhead and profit before assigning an advertising allowance.

For a lead-based business, use your own observed lead-to-customer rate when available. My suggested planning relationship is:

**Target cost per qualified lead = target customer acquisition cost × observed qualified-lead-to-customer rate.**

If you lack that history, label the rate as an assumption. Consider writing separate cautious and optimistic scenarios instead of presenting a guessed rate as established fact.

### How much evidence do you want to fund?

Choose a desired outcome: qualified inquiries, booked appointments or customers. Then use this planning relationship:

**Planned media budget = assumed media cost per outcome × desired outcome count.**

Write down why you chose that review point. A convenient outcome count should not become an automatic declaration that the campaign is profitable or ready to scale.

Check the proposed amount against your affordable loss limit. If it exceeds that limit, narrow the question, revise the experiment or wait until the business can fund it.

### What daily allowance follows from that plan?

For your own allocation worksheet, divide the planned media budget by the planned test duration. Treat this as a planning average; check the current budget guidance in Meta Ads Manager when configuring the campaign.

Before launch, record the total ceiling and review date. Avoid increasing the ceiling simply because the original experiment has become uncomfortable to evaluate.

## How should you budget for ad creative production?

Reserve production capacity before committing the full budget to distribution. Ask what each asset is intended to test: a customer problem, a solution, an offer or a reason to trust the business.

## What should you test when your budget is limited?

I recommend choosing one offer and one clearly described customer group for the initial brief. Write the question before making creative variations: “Will this message generate qualified inquiries within our acquisition target?”

Use awareness stages as prompts for distinct messages:

- **Problem aware:** describe the problem you want the reader to recognize.
- **Solution aware:** explain the approach you want them to consider.
- **Most aware:** present the offer and a clear next action.

For each creative, check the intended audience, specific promise, supportable wording, readable design and next step. Record the hypothesis beside the asset so your review has something concrete to assess.

## When should you increase, revise or stop spending?

At the planned review, compare actual qualified outcomes, full acquisition costs and your chosen affordability target. Include production and management expenses before deciding whether to fund another test.

My decision rule is straightforward: continue when the evidence supports the economics, revise when you can identify a specific unanswered question, and stop when the spending ceiling is reached without a defensible reason to continue.

If you suspect creative fatigue, write down that hypothesis and the evidence you would need to evaluate it. Keep a change log covering the offer, message, audience and destination, then give the next experiment a clear purpose.
